Technical notes · cfbmeta 1.1

CFB Meta Forecast

A weekly college football handicapping system that distills the public rating systems into one projected margin per game, grades it against the market, and — as of the first real backtest — reports honestly that it does not yet beat the closing line.

Season 2026 Tests 319 Backtest 6,936 games Status unvalidated
The headline result

Backtested over 6,936 games from 2021–2025, this model loses to the closing line: mean absolute error 15.25 points against the market's 12.08, and a 2709–2731–93 record against the spread — 49.8%, below the 52.4% needed to break even at −110. The fitted edge shrink came out at 0.000: none of its disagreement with the market carried signal.

That test is handicapped, and the caveat is real — it rates every game with the previous season's final ratings, while a live run uses ratings that have absorbed the season to date. So this is a floor, not a verdict. But it is the only measurement that currently exists, and until a better one does, the honest position is that nothing here should be sized with money.

Why the model cannot yet be tested properly

This is the central problem, and it is a data problem rather than a modelling one.

CFBD is a point-in-time API. /ratings/sp?year=2026 returns whatever SP+ is today. There is no as_of parameter, and last week's values are simply gone. So a backtest can rate a past game two ways, and both are wrong:

BasisProblemVerdict
same Season-final ratings already know how the games turned out lookahead
prior Last season's ratings; fair, but far weaker than a live run has a floor
snapshot What the ratings actually were that Thursday the real test

The snapshot basis cannot be reconstructed from the API. It can only be kept. So every run now archives what it saw: 

archive/2026/week-01/ratings.csv      team, source, rating — as they stood
                     lines.csv        consensus spread/total, book count, range
                     projections.csv  the full readout, gradeable after kickoff
                     meta.json        source provenance, config, timestamp

After the games, grade writes final scores and bet outcomes back into projections.csv. After a season of Thursdays, backtest --basis snapshot becomes possible — and that is the first result worth acting on. Until then the readout carries an UNVALIDATED banner stating the measured record in plain terms.

Consequence for the parameters

--write refuses to write edge_shrink from anything but a snapshot basis, because adopting 0.000 would set the operating value from the wrong experiment. Weights and sigma are written but inherit the same bias — the prior basis rewards whichever source is most forward-looking, which is why talent fitted so high (0.32) relative to its likely in-season value.

What the backtest did establish

Two results are basis-robust and worth keeping.

Key numbers, measured

The empirical margin distribution over 6,936 games is far spikier than the hand-picked defaults assumed:

Three and seven carry far more mass than assumed, and the gaps between key numbers are far emptier. This matters for any bet sitting on or near a number.

Calibration is directionally sound but overconfident in the middle

Predicted versus actual home win rate: the 60–80% and 80–100% buckets track well (69.1 → 69.2, 88.7 → 91.2). The 40–60% bucket predicted 52.5% and delivered 56.9%, meaning the model is underconfident on home teams in close games — consistent with the −1.82 point home bias in the same run.

Talent is built from the roster, not from signing classes

CFBD's packaged talent composite has no 2026 rows, so it is rebuilt from primary data — but from the current roster joined to each player's recruiting rating by athlete id, not from summing four signing classes. Summing classes counts players who transferred out, misses everyone who transferred in, and keeps players who left early. In the portal era those are not small corrections. A transfer appears on his new team's roster carrying the rating he signed with, so the join handles all three for free.

Top 40 roster spots by rating; unmatched spots (~42%: walk-ons, JUCO, international) imputed at the floor of the composite scale rather than dropped, since dropping them flatters teams with poor match coverage. Validated against CFBD's own published 2025 composite across 134 teams: Pearson r = 0.984, Spearman 0.991.

Three ways a source can fail, and all three are caught

  1. Missing — no rows. Obvious, drops out, weights renormalize.
  2. Flat — a row per team, all values identical, which is what a preseason SRS looks like. That is not a weak opinion but no opinion, and blending it drags every projection toward pick'em while looking healthy. Any source with under 10 rated teams or under half a point of spread is dropped.
  3. Stale — real, well-dispersed, outdated. Invisible in a single run, so each run fingerprints every source and records when those values first appeared. Unchanged for over a week gets flagged.

SP+ is checked against the source of record

CFBD mirrors SP+ and carries no version field, so a missed revision leaves no trace. ESPN's article HTML sits behind a WAF, but their content API serves the story, and its prose states year-over-year deltas — "Oklahoma State (up 22.0 points, 39th overall)" — which CFBD's own two seasons must reproduce. Against the 2026 final preseason article: 15 of 15 deltas match exactly. This runs weekly and reports in the footer.

The pipeline

  1. Each source proposes a neutral-field margin

    Home rating minus away rating. Missing sources drop out and weights renormalize.

  2. Blend by weight

    Weighted mean over the sources that actually showed up, with in-season sources downweighted in early weeks.

  3. Add home field once

    On top, not per source — every rating is quoted neutral-field, so per-source would multiply it.

  4. Apply situational adjustments

    Coaching, rest, travel. Each capped, then capped again in aggregate at ±4.

  5. Compare against the market

    The difference is the raw edge. What happens to it next is the most consequential decision in the system.

Home field, measured properly

The first version compared each team's mean home margin to its mean away margin, on the theory that team quality cancels. It does not — teams host the weak non-conference games and travel for the hard ones. That estimator returned 4.38 points across four seasons of FBS play, adding nearly two phantom points to every home team.

residual = actual_margin − (rating_home − rating_away) raw_hfa(t) = mean residual across t's home games hfa = (n · raw_hfa + 60 · league_mean) / (n + 60) clamped [0, 5]

Controlling for opponent strength gives 2.70, and conference-only games — where schedules are naturally balanced — independently agree at 2.58. After the fix the blend tracks the market at slope 0.995 with a mean absolute disagreement of 1.79 points.

Coaching, against roster talent

expected_sp = 8.20 · talent_z − 3.44 fitted, n = 890 team-seasons residual = actual_sp − expected_sp applied = cap · tanh(residual / 4) cap = 1.5

A team one standard deviation above average in roster talent is worth +8.2 SP+ points; the coach is credited with the residual, recency-weighted and shrunk by career length. The tanh matters: measured residuals run −6 to +14 against a 1.5 cap, so a hard clip gave a coach 13.9 points above expectation and one 5.2 above it the identical adjustment, flattening the entire top of the distribution.

Read the extremes with suspicion

Army (+13.9) and Air Force (+8.0) top the list, but the academies carry 6–16 rated recruits, so the talent metric floors them and the model expects disaster. Part of that residual is genuine overachievement and part is the talent measure failing on option programs. Cignetti (+12.4) and Sanders (−2.2) are the cleaner signals — both have normally-measured rosters.

Uncertainty and staking

Margins use σ = 19.32 (fitted, up from an assumed 16.0) over a discretized normal reweighted by the measured key numbers. Stakes are quarter-Kelly against a 100-unit bankroll at −110, capped at 3 units.

edge = projected_margin − market_margin settled = market_margin + edge · edge_shrink EV = p · 0.909 − (1 − p) kelly = (0.909 · p − q) / 0.909 q renormalized over non-push

edge_shrink is the fraction of our disagreement treated as real signal. Taking a raw disagreement at face value assumes the closing line is wrong by the full amount, which is not defensible for a model built from public ratings. It currently sits at an unvalidated 0.35; the only measurement so far returned 0.000.

What still stands between this and a legitimate model

In rough order of how much each one costs.

  1. It has never beaten the market on a fair test. Everything else is secondary. The snapshot archive is the fix, and it takes a season.
  2. Injuries and availability are entirely unmodelled. The single largest source of week-to-week variance in the sport, and a starting quarterback is worth more points than every adjustment in this system combined. No injury feed is wired in at all.
  3. Quarterback play is not separated from team rating. Related but distinct: a returning starter versus a first-time starter is a several-point swing that team-level ratings absorb slowly and preseason ratings guess at.
  4. No closing line value tracking. The system sees one line snapshot on Thursday. Whether our number moved toward or away from the close is the fastest available read on whether the edge is real — far faster than waiting on win/loss results — and it is not captured.
  5. No bet ledger. Recommendations are archived, but actual stakes, prices obtained, and results are not. Without the price you actually got, realised performance cannot be reconciled against modelled performance.
  6. Weather is ignored. Wind above roughly 15 mph is the single biggest weather effect on totals, and matters for spreads in extreme cases. CFBD exposes game weather.
  7. Bets are sized independently. Kelly assumes independence, but a slate is correlated — same conference, same weather, same market-wide mispricing. Ten "independent" quarter-Kelly bets is not quarter-Kelly risk.
  8. The market is one median, not a book. You bet a specific number at a specific book. A median across books can show an edge that does not exist at the price available to you, and ignores that the sharpest book's number is more informative than the softest.
  9. Situational spots are crude. Rest and travel are in; lookahead, letdown, rivalry, and coaching-staff changes below head coach are not.
  10. Sample size will remain brutal. Even a genuine 2-point edge needs hundreds of bets to distinguish from noise. One season will not settle it, and the temptation to conclude otherwise after a hot month is the main way models like this get people hurt.
The order to attack them

Closing line value first: it is cheap to add, uses data already being fetched, and gives a read on edge within weeks rather than seasons. Then injuries and quarterback status, which are the largest unmodelled effects. Then the bet ledger, so realised and modelled results can be reconciled. Correlation-aware sizing only matters once there is an edge worth sizing.

Credentials come from the environment or an untracked .env; real environment variables always win, so CI secrets are never shadowed. Email delivery is held behind a SEND_EMAIL repository variable — until it is set, the weekly workflow still builds the slate and uploads it as an artifact.

Two CFBD behaviours worth knowing

week=0 is treated as "no filter" and returns the entire season — a week-0 request came back with 1,638 games across weeks 1–15. The filter is re-applied client-side. And a CFBD week is not one playing weekend: week 1 of 2026 spans Aug 27 – Sep 7 and contains 124 teams twice, so the slate is also clipped to a date window anchored on the next kickoff.
