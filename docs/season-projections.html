Every FBS team's projected record, with the range it could plausibly land in — plus how badly the same method missed last year, so you know how much to argue with it.

How this works, in plain terms

The model has a rating for every team. For any matchup it turns two ratings into a predicted margin — say Georgia by 9. Then it plays the entire 2026 season 30,000 times.

Each time through, every game gets a random result centred on that predicted margin. Georgia by 9 doesn't mean Georgia wins by 9; it means Georgia wins by 9 on average, and any given Saturday could be a blowout or an upset. Count the wins, write them down, reset, play the season again.

Do that 30,000 times and you don't get one prediction — you get a distribution. Arkansas comes out at 4.4 wins, but that's the average of 30,000 seasons in which they won anywhere from 1 to 9.

The one non-obvious part. Each simulated season also rolls a single "we might just be wrong about this team" adjustment per team, held across all their games. Without it the ranges come out far too narrow. Random game-to-game luck averages out over a season; being wrong about how good a team is does not — that error shows up in all twelve games the same direction. That correction is why these ranges are as wide as they are, and it was tuned until the ranges were honest.

Two wins off per team is a lot, and it is the honest number. If your gut and this table disagree by a win or two, that is noise — your gut is as likely to be right. Disagree by four or more and one of you is seeing something the other isn't.

The 80% ranges covered 74% rather than 80%, so they run slightly too narrow even after correction. Widen them a touch in your head. One caveat in the model's favour: the 2025 test could only use 2024's final ratings, while the 2026 numbers below have real preseason ratings that already account for who returned and who was recruited.

What that tells you

The big misses are mostly teams that changed character in one offseason — a new quarterback, a portal haul, a coaching change that took. Ratings built on last year's results cannot see that coming, and neither can anything else in August.

Which is the practical point: where you have real information the model can't have — a quarterback situation, an injury, a locker room — your prior should beat this table. Where you don't, it probably shouldn't.
