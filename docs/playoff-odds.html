How the field is chosen

Each simulated season runs to the end, then conference championship games are played between the top two in each league. That settles who the champions are.

A model of the selection committee then ranks everyone. The five highest-ranked champions take automatic bids; the next seven teams of any kind fill the field. The twelve are seeded strictly by that ranking, so the four byes go to the four best teams — champion or not.

First round is 5v12, 6v11, 7v10, 8v9 at the higher seed's home field. Everything after that is neutral-site.

The committee is the hard part

Everything above is mechanical once you know the ranking — and the ranking is a room full of people, not a formula. Rather than invent weights, the model is fit to twelve seasons of final CFP rankings (2014–2025, 300 ranked teams), learning how record, quality and schedule strength actually translate into a ranking.

Translated into rating points so it can be argued with:

worth +4.7 rating points

That a loss costs about three times what a win pays is the most important fact about how this committee behaves, and it is not something a hand-tuned formula would have guessed.

Held out one season at a time, the model reproduces the real committee ranking at 0.89 Spearman, placing 10.8 of the actual top twelve and 3.4 of the actual top four in the right group.

Where it broke in 2025

Given real 2025 records and real champions, the model picks 10 of the 12 teams that actually made it, and 3 of the 4 byes. It took BYU (11–2) and Notre Dame (10–2); the committee took Alabama (10–3) and Miami (10–2).

That is the model's blind spot in one line. It counts losses; the committee also weighs who you are. A three-loss Alabama got in over a two-loss BYU, and no model built from records and ratings is going to predict that. Expect these numbers to understate blue bloods on the bubble and overstate everyone else.

What not to do with this

These odds inherit every weakness of the ratings underneath them. That model is about two wins off per team on a season-long projection, and it has never been shown to beat a betting market on a single game.

So: a team at 45% and a team at 55% are not meaningfully different. A team at 5% and a team at 50% are. Read the big gaps, ignore the small ones, and treat anything on the bubble as genuinely uncertain rather than as a number with a decimal point.
